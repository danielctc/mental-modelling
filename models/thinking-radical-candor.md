# Radical Candor

## Trigger Card

Before giving feedback, check which quadrant you're actually in:

1. **Do I care personally?** Have I shown this person I'm on their side?
2. **Am I challenging directly?** Am I saying the actual thing, or a softened version?
3. **Both → Radical Candor.** Care only → **Ruinous Empathy**. Challenge only → **Obnoxious Aggression**. Neither → **Manipulative Insincerity**.
4. **The default failure is ruinous empathy** — being nice instead of clear.

Use before any difficult conversation. Takes seconds.

## Overview

Kim Scott's framework, from managing at Google and Apple. The two axes are independent, which is the point: most people treat kindness and honesty as a trade-off and pick one.

```
                    CHALLENGE DIRECTLY
                          ▲
      OBNOXIOUS           │        RADICAL
      AGGRESSION          │        CANDOR
      "Brutal honesty"    │        "Here's the problem,
      Right, but cruel    │         and I'm on your side"
   ───────────────────────┼───────────────────────▶ CARE PERSONALLY
      MANIPULATIVE        │        RUINOUS
      INSINCERITY         │        EMPATHY
      Backstabbing,       │        "It's fine, honestly"
      political silence   │        Kind, and useless
```

**Core Principle:** Care personally AND challenge directly. Not one or the other, and not a compromise between them.

The framework tells you *whether to speak and in what spirit*. `thinking-situation-behavior-impact` tells you *what words to use*. Use both.

## When to Use

- Performance conversations and 1:1s
- Someone is underperforming and you've been putting it off (that delay is the diagnosis)
- Reviewing work you think is weak
- Peer feedback where you have no authority
- Checking your own motives before a hard conversation
- Diagnosing why past feedback didn't land

## When NOT to Use

- **You don't have a relationship yet.** Radical candor is earned — it is measured at the listener's ear, not the speaker's mouth. Direct challenge without demonstrated care reads as obnoxious aggression no matter how you meant it.
- **Power imbalance downward without trust.** A new manager going straight to direct challenge does damage.
- **As cover for being harsh.** "I'm just being radically candid" is the single most common misuse and it is always obnoxious aggression wearing a badge.
- **Public settings.** Praise in public; challenge in private.
- **Safeguarding, HR, or legal issues.** Different process.

## The Four Quadrants

### Radical Candor — care + challenge
You say the real thing, and the person can tell you want them to succeed. Feels uncomfortable in the moment and good afterwards.

### Ruinous Empathy — care, no challenge
The most common failure, and the most expensive. You like them, you don't want to upset them, so you soften it until nothing is transmitted. They find out six months later in a review, or when they're let go, and the kindest thing you did turns out to have been the cruellest.

**Symptoms:** feedback that starts with "this is only small"; feedback sandwiches; "how are you finding things?" instead of "here's what isn't working".

### Obnoxious Aggression — challenge, no care
Correct and unusable. Sometimes gets a short-term result, always costs trust. Better than ruinous empathy in one narrow sense — at least the information got through — but it doesn't survive repetition.

### Manipulative Insincerity — neither
Political silence, backstabbing, faint praise, agreeing in the room and briefing against afterwards. The worst quadrant and the hardest to admit to.

## The Process

### Step 1: Check the care axis honestly
Not "do I care" — **have I demonstrated it, recently, in a way they'd recognise?** Care is evidenced by time given, credit given, and problems solved for them.

### Step 2: Check the challenge axis
Write the feedback in one sentence. Then ask: is this the actual thing, or the acceptable version of it? If you've hedged it into ambiguity, you're in ruinous empathy.

### Step 3: Solicit before you give
Scott's ordering: ask for feedback on yourself first, and act on it visibly. It proves the axis and it makes the next conversation two-way rather than a verdict.

### Step 4: Give it — using SBI
Radical Candor is the posture; `thinking-situation-behavior-impact` is the script. Situation, observed behaviour, impact, ask about intent.

### Step 5: Check where it landed
Ask. "Did that land the way I meant it?" The quadrant is determined by their experience, not your intention — which means you cannot self-certify.

## Application Patterns

### The delay test
If you've been putting a conversation off for more than two weeks, you are in ruinous empathy. The delay is the symptom. The fix is not more preparation.

### Praise, done properly
The framework applies to praise too. "Great work" is ruinous empathy's cheerful twin — no information. Radically candid praise is specific enough to repeat: what they did, and why it mattered.

### Receiving it
Ask for it, don't argue with it in the moment, and reward the candour visibly — otherwise you'll only get ruinous empathy back forever.

## Verification Checklist
- [ ] Care demonstrated recently in a way they'd actually name
- [ ] Feedback written as one direct sentence, unhedged
- [ ] Not delayed past two weeks
- [ ] Delivered privately (challenge) / publicly (praise)
- [ ] Scripted with SBI rather than adjectives about the person
- [ ] Checked afterwards where it landed for them

## Key Questions
- "Am I being kind, or am I being clear?"
- "Have I actually shown this person I'm on their side?"
- "Is this the real feedback, or the acceptable version?"
- "How long have I been putting this off, and why?"
- "Would they describe this as candour or as an attack?"
