# Nonviolent Communication (NVC)

## Trigger Card

When a conversation is charged and you're about to say something that will land badly:

1. **Observation** — what happened, camera-level, no evaluation.
2. **Feeling** — what you feel. A real feeling, not a disguised judgement.
3. **Need** — the underlying need that feeling points to.
4. **Request** — a specific, present, doable ask. Not a demand.

Skip for routine, low-emotion feedback — SBI is lighter and faster there.

## Overview

Marshall Rosenberg's framework. Its claim: most conflict comes from expressing needs as judgements about other people. "You're inconsiderate" is a need for consideration, encoded as an attack — and encoded that way it cannot be met, only defended against.

The four components normally arrive fused into one sentence, and fusing them is what makes them unusable.

```
FUSED:      "You never respect my time. It's incredibly selfish."

SEPARATED:  O — "The last three 1:1s started fifteen minutes late."
            F — "I feel frustrated"
            N — "because I need to be able to plan my afternoon."
            R — "Would you be willing to move it to 3pm, or tell me
                 by noon if it's going to slip?"
```

**Core Principle:** Separate observation from evaluation, and express the need rather than the judgement.

## When to Use

- Emotionally charged conversations — yours or someone else's
- Recurring friction that ordinary feedback hasn't shifted
- Conflict where both sides have dug in
- When you notice you're building a case against someone in your head
- Personal relationships, not just work
- Understanding what someone else actually needs when they're being difficult

## When NOT to Use

- **Routine work feedback.** `thinking-situation-behavior-impact` is lighter and doesn't require anyone to discuss feelings. Use NVC when the emotional layer is genuinely load-bearing.
- **Fast operational conversations.** Four components mid-incident is wrong.
- **A culture that will find it strange.** The structure can sound therapeutic and land as manipulative. Use the *thinking* without the visible scaffolding — internally separate the four, then speak normally.
- **Genuine abuse, bullying or safeguarding.** NVC is not a substitute for a formal process, and framing abuse as an unmet need is a serious error.
- **As a technique to win.** NVC used instrumentally is manipulative insincerity with better vocabulary.

## The Four Components

### 1. Observation, free of evaluation
The hardest part. Rosenberg: *"Observing without evaluating is the highest form of human intelligence."*

| Evaluation (disguised as observation) | Observation |
|---|---|
| "You're always late" | "The last three meetings started 15 minutes late" |
| "The work was sloppy" | "The PR had no tests and three lint failures" |
| "You ignored me" | "I asked twice and didn't get a reply" |

Test: could a camera have recorded it? Words like *always, never, too, lazy, unprofessional* are evaluations.

### 2. Feeling, not a disguised judgement
Real feelings: frustrated, anxious, disappointed, relieved, tired, hurt.

**Pseudo-feelings** — judgements wearing a feeling's clothes, because they describe what you think the *other person did*:
- "I feel ignored" → that's *they ignored me*. Feeling: hurt, lonely.
- "I feel manipulated" → *they manipulated me*. Feeling: uneasy, mistrustful.
- "I feel disrespected" → *they disrespected me*. Feeling: angry, diminished.

Anything of the form "I feel [that you...]" is a thought, not a feeling.

### 3. Need
Feelings point at needs. Universal human needs: clarity, respect, autonomy, rest, competence, connection, safety, contribution, predictability.

Say the need, not the strategy for meeting it. "I need you to reply within an hour" is a strategy. "I need to know where things stand" is the need — and it has many possible strategies, which is what makes agreement possible.

### 4. Request, not demand
- **Specific:** "be more communicative" isn't actionable. "Post a line in the channel when you're blocked" is.
- **Positive:** what you *do* want, not what you want stopped.
- **Present:** doable now or next time, not a personality change.
- **A request, not a demand:** the test is what you do when they say no. If refusal earns punishment or sulking, it was a demand and they knew it before you did.

## Application Patterns

### Receiving hostility
NVC runs in reverse: hear the need under the attack.
```
They say:  "You clearly don't care about this project."
Hear:      Observation? (deadlines slipped)
           Feeling?     (anxious, unsupported)
           Need?        (reliability, being kept informed)
Respond:   "Are you worried this is going to slip again and you won't
            hear about it until it has?"
```
Guessing the need out loud de-escalates faster than defending yourself, even when you guess wrong — being asked about is the point.

### Internal use only
In a workplace where the full form would sound odd, do the four steps in your head and then speak normally. You'll still say something materially different from what you'd have said — usually shorter and much less accusatory.

### Self-connection
Turn it inward when you're stuck. What did I observe? What am I feeling? What need isn't met? What's the request — of myself or someone else?

## Verification Checklist
- [ ] Observation would survive a camera check — no *always/never/too*
- [ ] Feeling is a feeling, not "I feel that you..."
- [ ] Need stated as a need, not as the strategy for meeting it
- [ ] Request is specific, positive, present and doable
- [ ] I'm genuinely prepared for "no"
- [ ] Not being used to win an argument

## Key Questions
- "What did the camera see?"
- "Is that a feeling, or a judgement in disguise?"
- "What need of mine is unmet here?"
- "Am I asking for a need or dictating a strategy?"
- "What will I do if they say no?"
- "What might *their* unmet need be?"
