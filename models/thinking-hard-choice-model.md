# Hard Choice Model

## Trigger Card

When a decision is taking longer than it should:

1. **Impact:** how significant is the outcome? High or low?
2. **Comparability:** how easy is it to compare the options? Easy or hard?
3. **Read the quadrant** and take its approach.
4. **Heuristic:** the bigger the impact, the more carefully you proceed — and no further.

Always worth 30 seconds before any decision that's stalling.

## Overview

A meta-decision tool: it tells you *how much decision-making to do*. Most wasted decision effort comes from misclassification — agonising over no-brainers, and rushing hard choices because they're uncomfortable.

```
                 EASY TO COMPARE        HARD TO COMPARE
              ┌────────────────────┬────────────────────┐
   HIGH       │ BIG CHOICE         │ HARD CHOICE        │
   IMPACT     │ Gather info,       │ No right answer.   │
              │ compare thoroughly │ Evaluate factors,  │
              │                    │ run an experiment  │
              ├────────────────────┼────────────────────┤
   LOW        │ NO-BRAINER         │ APPLES & ORANGES   │
   IMPACT     │ Decide now.        │ Decide on current  │
              │ Optimising is      │ priorities. Don't  │
              │ waste              │ over-analyse       │
              └────────────────────┴────────────────────┘
```

**Core Principle:** Match decision effort to decision type. Most people get this backwards.

## When to Use

- You've been going round on something for longer than it deserves
- Before opening a spreadsheet to compare options
- Career and life decisions
- Any decision where you're not sure whether to just pick one
- Coaching someone who's stuck

## When NOT to Use

- **The decision is already made** and you're seeking permission.
- **You're using "hard choice" to avoid deciding.** The model's advice for hard choices is to accept there's no right answer and move — not to keep deliberating.
- Trivially urgent decisions — act, then classify if it recurs.

## The Four Types

### 1. No-brainer — low impact, easy to compare
Meeting slot. Template choice. Which library for a throwaway script.

**Approach:** decide now. Optimising here is pure waste. If you're stuck, flip a coin — that's a legitimate answer at this quadrant.

### 2. Apples and oranges — low impact, hard to compare
Read or exercise tonight? Conference or a course? Options differ in kind, but the stakes are low.

**Approach:** decide on current priorities. What do you need *this week*? Don't build a matrix for these — a total score across incomparable things is false precision.

### 3. Big choice — high impact, easy to compare
Two job offers with stated salaries. Two flats with prices and locations. Two vendors with published pricing and features.

**Approach:** this is where thorough work pays. Gather information, build confidence, use `thinking-decision-matrix`. The options are comparable, so comparison genuinely resolves it.

### 4. Hard choice — high impact, hard to compare
Senior role at a big company vs early-stage startup. IC track vs management. Stay or leave.

**Approach:** accept there is no right answer — comparison won't resolve it because the options are incommensurable. Then:
- Evaluate the factors anyway (a matrix here clarifies the trade-off even when it can't pick a winner)
- **Run a small experiment** if one exists — a secondment, a trial project, a month
- Use `thinking-regret-minimisation` — which will 80-year-old you wish you'd tried?
- Decide, commit, and stop revisiting

## Application Patterns

### The common failure
```
Reality:      No-brainer          Treated as:  Big choice
              (which CI runner)                (three days of benchmarking)

Reality:      Hard choice         Treated as:  No-brainer
              (take the role?)                 (decided in an evening because
                                                thinking about it felt bad)
```

Discomfort drives both errors: no-brainers are comfortable to analyse, hard choices are not.

### Reversibility overlay
Cross-reference with `thinking-reversibility`. A high-impact, hard-to-compare decision that is *reversible* is much less frightening than it feels — and the right approach is usually "run the experiment", because you can come back.

## Verification Checklist
- [ ] Impact judged honestly (what changes in a year?)
- [ ] Comparability judged (are these the same kind of thing?)
- [ ] Effort matched to the quadrant
- [ ] No-brainers actually decided immediately
- [ ] For hard choices: experiment considered, then committed to
- [ ] Reversibility checked before treating it as terminal

## Key Questions
- "How much will this actually change in a year?"
- "Are these options even the same kind of thing?"
- "Am I optimising a no-brainer because the real decision is uncomfortable?"
- "Is there a small experiment that would tell me more than more thinking would?"
- "Can I undo this?"
